# What MVP Development Actually Costs (and Why the Range Is So Wide)

Ask three development shops to quote the same idea and you'll often get three numbers that don't seem to describe the same project — one at $15,000, one at $60,000, one at $150,000. Founders usually assume this means someone is either overcharging or cutting corners. Sometimes that's true. More often, the three quotes are actually pricing three different projects, because "MVP" was never precisely defined before the quote was requested.

This article explains what actually drives MVP cost, so you can tell the difference between a quote that's wrong and a quote that's pricing something you didn't ask for.

## Why the same idea produces wildly different quotes

A pitch like "a marketplace connecting X and Y" can mean a weekend prototype with no payments and fake data, or it can mean real-time messaging, escrow payments, dispute handling, ratings, and admin moderation tools. Both are technically "an MVP" of the same idea. The cost gap between them is not a pricing disagreement — it's a scope disagreement that nobody surfaced before quotes went out.

The single biggest lever on cost is not the technology stack. It's how many decisions have already been made before development starts. A founder who arrives with wireframes, a written spec of core workflows, and clear answers about who the first users are will get tighter, more comparable quotes than one who arrives with a one-paragraph pitch — because the development shop isn't guessing at scope anymore.

## The real cost drivers, in order of impact

**1. Number of distinct user roles and permission levels.** A product with one type of user (everyone sees the same thing) is meaningfully cheaper than one with three roles (admin, team member, external client) that each see different views and can take different actions. Every additional role multiplies the number of states your interface and backend need to handle correctly.

**2. Real-time or collaborative features.** Live chat, live document editing, real-time notifications, and multi-user concurrent workflows require infrastructure and testing that simple CRUD screens don't. If your MVP genuinely needs this on day one, budget for it directly; if it's "nice to have," it's one of the easiest things to defer.

**3. Payments and billing complexity.** Taking a single one-time payment via Stripe Checkout is a day of work. Usage-based billing, proration, multiple pricing tiers with feature gating, and dunning logic for failed payments is a project in itself. Founders frequently underestimate this because "add Stripe" sounds like one line item.

**4. Third-party integrations.** Every external system you connect to — a CRM, an accounting tool, a shipping API, a legacy system with undocumented behavior — adds integration and error-handling work that's hard to estimate without reading that system's actual documentation, or in some cases, without a discovery spike to test it directly.

**5. Data migration or import from an existing system.** If you're replacing a spreadsheet-based process or an old tool, the effort of mapping and cleaning that existing data is often comparable to building the new features themselves.

**6. Design maturity.** Building from finished, reviewed designs is faster and more predictable than building while design decisions are still being made mid-sprint. This isn't about spending months on design — a few days of focused UX work on your core flows pays for itself in development speed.

## What a realistic cost range looks like

For a genuinely minimal single-role SaaS MVP — one core workflow, simple auth, basic billing, no real-time features — a realistic range for production-quality custom development is roughly $20,000–$45,000, delivered over eight to twelve weeks with an experienced team.

For a multi-role B2B product with a handful of integrations and tiered billing, expect $50,000–$120,000 and a twelve to twenty week timeline.

For products with real-time collaboration, complex payments logic, or multiple external integrations that need custom handling, $120,000 and up is realistic, and the timeline depends heavily on how many of those complex pieces run in parallel versus sequence.

These ranges assume production-quality work: real error handling, real data isolation between accounts, a deployment pipeline, and code a second engineer could maintain. Numbers well below these ranges usually mean either a much smaller scope than described, an offshore team at a very different cost structure with corresponding communication and quality tradeoffs, or a build that will need significant rework before it can safely take paying customers.

## Fixed price vs. time-and-materials

Fixed-price quotes work well when the scope is genuinely well-defined — you know the screens, the roles, the integrations, and they're unlikely to change mid-project. The risk is that any change request outside that scope becomes a change order, which can feel adversarial if the original scope wasn't tight.

Time-and-materials (or a retainer with a not-to-exceed cap) works better for MVPs where you expect to learn and adjust as you go, which is most MVPs. You trade cost certainty for flexibility. The way to make this safe is weekly or biweekly checkpoints with visible progress and a running total, so "time and materials" doesn't become a euphemism for unbounded spend.

Neither model fixes a bad scoping process. A fixed price on a vague scope just moves the argument to change orders. Time-and-materials on a vague scope just moves the argument to "why is this taking so long."

## Common mistakes that inflate cost or cause rework

- **Requesting quotes before deciding who the first users are.** Roles and permissions drive cost more than almost anything else, and you can't scope them without knowing who's using the product.
- **Treating the designer and developer as separate, sequential steps with no overlap.** Design decisions made in isolation from technical constraints often need rework once development starts.
- **Skipping a discovery phase to save money upfront.** A one- to two-week [discovery process](/process) typically costs far less than the rework caused by a spec that changes significantly once development is underway.
- **Comparing quotes without comparing scope documents.** Ask every bidder for a written breakdown of what's included, not just a total number. This is the fastest way to see whether quotes are actually comparable.
- **Optimizing entirely for the lowest number.** The cheapest bid is sometimes genuinely efficient. It's also sometimes a smaller scope in disguise, or a team that will be more expensive to work with once you count your own time spent managing quality issues.

## When it's worth spending more upfront

If your product's core value depends entirely on one workflow being fast, reliable, and pleasant to use — the thing that makes someone come back tomorrow — that workflow deserves more design and engineering attention than the rest of the MVP, even if it costs more. Everything adjacent to it (settings pages, admin tools, secondary features) can be rougher without hurting adoption. Spreading budget evenly across every screen usually means the one screen that matters most gets the same average effort as the ones that don't.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it cheaper to hire a freelancer than an agency or studio?**
Sometimes, for narrow, well-defined scopes where you can manage the project yourself. For a full MVP with multiple moving parts, the coordination and quality-control cost of managing several freelancers often erases the savings versus a small dedicated team.

**Should I get a fixed quote before or after a discovery phase?**
After, if you want a quote you can rely on. A quote given before discovery is really a rough estimate based on incomplete information — useful for a gut check, not for a budget commitment.

**Does using a low-code tool reduce cost significantly?**
For very simple products, yes. Once you need custom business logic, non-trivial permissions, or integrations the tool doesn't support natively, low-code platforms often cost more in workarounds than a lean custom build would have cost directly.

**What's a reasonable ongoing cost after the MVP launches?**
Budget roughly 15–20% of the initial build cost annually for maintenance, security updates, and small improvements, more if you're actively iterating on features based on user feedback.

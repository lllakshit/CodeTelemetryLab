# What a Website Maintenance Plan Should Actually Cover

"Website maintenance" gets sold as a monthly retainer so often, and so vaguely, that many small business owners assume it means "we'll fix things if they break" without ever seeing what's actually included. That vagueness is a problem, because a real maintenance plan and a padded one look identical on an invoice and very different in practice, usually only becoming obvious the day something actually breaks and there's no clear plan for who fixes it or how fast.

## What genuine maintenance actually includes

**Dependency and security updates.** Every website built with a framework, CMS, or plugin ecosystem depends on external code that receives security patches over time. Applying these updates regularly — and testing that nothing breaks when you do — is unglamorous, recurring work that's easy to skip and expensive to have skipped when a known vulnerability in an outdated dependency eventually gets exploited.

**Uptime and error monitoring.** Someone or something should notice when your site goes down or starts throwing errors, ideally before your customers do. This requires actual monitoring tooling configured to alert a real person, not a vague promise to "keep an eye on it."

**Backup verification, not just backup existence.** Having backups configured means nothing if nobody has verified a backup can actually be restored successfully. A maintenance plan worth paying for periodically tests restoration, not just confirms backups are being taken.

**Performance monitoring over time.** Sites tend to slow down gradually as content, images, and third-party scripts accumulate. Periodic performance review catches this drift before it becomes a noticeable problem for visitors.

**Small content and functionality updates.** Text changes, image swaps, minor layout adjustments, and small bug fixes that don't warrant a full project engagement but do need someone with access and the right skills to handle promptly.

**A defined response time for something breaking.** This is the part that matters most and gets specified least. "We'll get to it" is not a maintenance plan. A real plan states a response time commitment for different severity levels — a fully broken site is different from a minor visual glitch, and the plan should say so explicitly.

## What maintenance plans often quietly exclude, and should be explicit about

**New feature development.** A maintenance retainer covering upkeep of what exists is a different scope than ongoing feature development. Both are reasonable to want, but they should be priced and scoped separately, or you'll find yourself either overpaying for maintenance that's mostly used for new work, or frustrated that "maintenance" doesn't cover the feature request you assumed it would.

**Major version upgrades of underlying frameworks.** Routine dependency patches are standard maintenance. A major framework version upgrade (a significant React version bump, a CMS major version migration) is often a distinct, larger project, and a maintenance plan should be clear about which category it falls into.

**Hosting and infrastructure costs themselves.** Maintenance retainers typically cover the labor of managing your hosting, not the hosting bill itself, which is usually a separate, pass-through cost.

## What a reasonable cost range looks like

For a straightforward small business website — informational pages, maybe a contact form, no complex custom functionality — a reasonable maintenance retainer is often in the range of $150–$500 a month, covering security updates, monitoring, backups, and a modest allowance of small content changes.

For a site with more custom functionality, integrations, or e-commerce components, $500–$1,500 a month is more realistic, scaling with how much custom code needs ongoing attention and how much small-change volume you actually need.

Retainers well below these ranges are worth scrutinizing closely for what's actually included — genuine security monitoring and tested backups have a real labor cost that a very cheap retainer likely isn't covering.

## How to evaluate a maintenance plan before signing

Ask directly: what's the response time commitment if the site goes down entirely, versus a minor bug? How often are backups tested, not just taken? What specifically counts as included small changes, and what's billed separately? Who do we contact, and what's the actual escalation path if something urgent happens on a weekend? A provider who answers these specifically, rather than with reassuring generalities, is more likely to actually deliver the maintenance you're paying for.

## When you might not need a formal maintenance plan

If your website is simple, rarely changes, and you have some internal capability (even just comfort with basic CMS updates) to handle minor issues, a formal ongoing retainer may be more than you need. An occasional check-in or pay-as-needed arrangement for updates can be more cost-effective for genuinely low-maintenance sites. The calculation changes once your site involves custom functionality, handles customer data, or processes payments — at that point the security and reliability stakes justify more structured ongoing attention.

## Common mistakes

- **Signing a maintenance retainer without a written definition of response times and included scope**, then discovering during an actual emergency that expectations don't match reality.
- **Treating maintenance as optional to save money**, then facing a much larger cost when a security vulnerability in an unpatched dependency causes a real incident.
- **Not verifying backups are actually restorable** until the day you desperately need to restore one and discover it doesn't work.
- **Bundling maintenance and new feature work into one vague retainer** with no clear boundary, making it hard to evaluate whether you're getting good value from either.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should security updates actually happen?**
Critical security patches should be applied promptly, ideally within days of release for anything flagged as high severity, not batched into a quarterly update cycle. Routine, non-critical updates can reasonably happen on a monthly cadence.

**What happens if we want to switch maintenance providers?**
You should have full ownership of your code, hosting accounts, and domain registration regardless of who's doing maintenance, so switching should mean handing off documentation and access, not migrating anything. If a provider makes switching difficult, that's worth addressing before you're in that situation, not after.

**Is it normal for maintenance retainers to roll over unused hours?**
This varies by provider. Some offer rollover for unused small-change allowances, others don't. Clarify this upfront since it materially affects the value of the retainer if your actual monthly need for small changes fluctuates.

**Do we need maintenance if our site was built on a platform like Squarespace or Shopify?**
Less than a fully custom site, since the platform handles core security patching, but you likely still benefit from monitoring, performance review, and someone available for content or configuration changes.

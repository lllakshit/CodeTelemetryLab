# Why We Run a Discovery Phase Before Writing MVP Code

A founder came to us with what sounded like a clear brief: a booking platform for independent tutors, similar in spirit to how service marketplaces work, but for one-on-one lessons. Two weeks into discovery, it turned out the actual hard problem wasn't the booking calendar — it was that tutors needed to see a student's learning history across multiple past sessions with different tutors, something the original brief never mentioned because the founder assumed it was obvious. That single requirement changed the data model, which changed the API design, which changed the estimate by several weeks.

This is the case for discovery: not as a sales step before "real work" begins, but as the phase where the requirements that actually determine architecture get surfaced, before they're expensive to change.

## What discovery is actually for

Discovery is not a requirements-gathering formality. It has three specific jobs:

**Surface the requirements the founder didn't know were requirements.** Most founders deeply understand their market and their users' pain points, but haven't translated that into the level of detail a data model needs. "Tutors need to see student history" sounds like a UI detail. It's actually a data modeling decision about whether sessions belong to a tutor-student relationship or to a student independent of any specific tutor.

**Find the disagreements before they're expensive.** If a founder and their co-founder have different mental models of how the product works — one thinks in terms of projects, the other thinks in terms of clients — that disagreement is cheap to resolve on a whiteboard and expensive to resolve after both models are half-built into the codebase.

**Produce something the team can actually estimate against.** "Build a booking platform" cannot be accurately estimated. A list of specific user flows, data entities, and integration points can be. Discovery converts an idea into something a development team can size honestly instead of guessing.

## What a useful discovery phase actually produces

A discovery phase that's earning its cost produces concrete artifacts, not just meeting notes:

- **A written map of core user flows** — the three to six things a user does that make the product valuable, described step by step, including edge cases like "what happens if a booking is cancelled less than 24 hours before the session."
- **A data model sketch** — the core entities (user, organization, booking, session) and how they relate, reviewed specifically for the edge cases that tend to be expensive to retrofit, like multi-user access and historical data relationships.
- **An explicit list of what's in scope for v1 and what's deliberately deferred**, with the reasoning written down so it's a decision, not something that gets silently forgotten and then reintroduced as a surprise mid-build.
- **Open questions with owners** — things that genuinely can't be answered yet (like exact payment provider requirements) assigned to someone with a deadline, rather than left ambiguous.
- **A rough architecture direction** — not a final tech stack decision necessarily, but enough clarity on complexity (real-time features, integrations, data volume) to size the project honestly.

If a discovery process doesn't produce artifacts like these, it's either too short to be useful or too long and consuming budget that should go toward building.

## How long discovery should take

For a straightforward MVP with a single core workflow, one to two weeks of focused discovery is usually enough — a handful of working sessions plus time to synthesize findings into the artifacts above. For a multi-role platform with several integrations or an unclear data model, two to four weeks is reasonable. Beyond four weeks, discovery is usually either poorly scoped itself or being used as a way to avoid committing to a build decision.

The right amount of discovery is inversely related to how clear the founder's mental model already is. A founder who's run the manual version of this process for months — doing bookings by hand, tracking client history in a spreadsheet — often needs less discovery time than one starting from a purely theoretical idea, because the real-world edge cases are already known from experience.

## Tradeoffs and when to skip it

Discovery isn't free, and it's not always the right call. If you're building a genuinely simple tool with one user type and no ambiguity in the core workflow — a form that captures data and displays a report, for instance — a lightweight kickoff conversation may cover everything a formal discovery phase would, and adding a two-week phase on top of that is padding, not value.

Discovery also isn't a substitute for talking to real users. It organizes and pressure-tests what you already know; it doesn't manufacture product-market fit. If your biggest open question is "will anyone actually want this," that's a customer conversation problem, not a discovery workshop problem — spend that time talking to prospective users before spending it in architecture sessions.

## Common mistakes we see

- **Treating discovery as a sales formality to get through quickly.** If the team running discovery isn't asking hard, sometimes uncomfortable questions about edge cases and assumptions, it's not doing its job.
- **Skipping discovery to "save time," then paying for it later** in the form of a data model rewrite three months into the build, once the real requirements surface anyway — just at a much more expensive point in the process.
- **Running discovery without involving whoever will actually build the product.** Requirements gathered by a salesperson or project manager and handed to engineers secondhand tend to lose the technical nuance that matters most.
- **Producing a discovery document nobody references again.** The output should be a living reference the team returns to during development, not a report that gets filed away after the kickoff call.

## What happens after discovery

A good discovery phase ends with a scope and rough estimate the team is confident in, not because the estimate is padded for safety, but because the unknowns that usually cause estimate drift have already been resolved. From there, the transition into [MVP development](/services/mvp-development) should feel continuous — the same understanding of the product carries forward, rather than discovery being handed off to a different team that starts from scratch.

If your idea is closer to our tutoring platform example — clear in your head, but not yet translated into something a team can build against — that's exactly the gap a discovery phase closes. ## Frequently asked questions

**Do we need discovery if we already have detailed wireframes?**
Wireframes describe the interface, not necessarily the data model or edge cases behind it. Discovery can be shorter if wireframes exist, but it's still worth a focused session to pressure-test what happens in the flows wireframes don't show — errors, edge cases, and permission boundaries.

**Is discovery billed separately from development?**
Usually yes, as a distinct phase with its own fixed scope, since its deliverable (a validated plan) is different from development's deliverable (working software). This also gives you a natural decision point to confirm the plan before committing to the full build.

**What if discovery reveals the idea needs to change significantly?**
That's discovery working as intended, not a failure. Finding out a core assumption doesn't hold before spending months building against it is the entire point.

**Can we run discovery ourselves and just hire developers for the build?**
Yes, if you have someone internally with the experience to ask the right architectural questions and document decisions clearly. The risk is not knowing which questions matter until a development team surfaces the gaps mid-build — which is the exact cost discovery is meant to avoid.
